# Credit Downgrade Hits Bond Markets - 30yr Yield hits 5%

## 1. Thesis

The Moody's downgrade pushing the 30-year back to 5% matters less for the round number than for its composition: the last 5.1% print in late 2023 came with roughly 3% real growth and 3.6% CPI to justify higher nominal yields, while today's 5% comes with roughly 2% growth and 2.3% CPI — so the rise is a higher term premium for fiscal risk, not stronger fundamentals. With nominal growth slipping back below the cost of debt, the 2021-2023 financial-repression window that cut debt-to-GDP from 133% to 115% has reversed toward 121%, and each new deficit dollar buys less GDP. More supply from an 11%-wider FY2025 deficit plus foreign repatriation since February hits the long end hardest, producing a bear steepener the presenter expects Treasury to fund around by staying heavy in bills.

## 4B TAKE

- [TAKE] [03:45] The 30-year rises while growth and inflation fall because the premium above inflation, measured as nominal 10-year minus expected inflation, follows the long yield higher.
- [TAKE] [08:30] Debt to GDP falls when nominal growth exceeds the interest rate, as in the mid-1990s and the post-2020 rebound, and rises when yields exceed nominal growth, as now.
- [TAKE] [22:23] A larger deficit means more bond supply that lowers bond prices for constant demand, while trade upheaval and capital repatriation since February lower demand, especially for long bonds.
- [TAKE] [24:45] Long duration demands a larger inflation and fiscal premium because a 2-year holder risks one or two hot years while a 30-year holder risks a full inflate-away cycle.
- [TAKE] [25:00] The government wants nominal GDP above borrowing cost through negative real rates, but the market remembers 2021 losses near 6% per year and demands higher compensation upfront.
