# Uncovering Trillions in Cross-Border Flows - Shocking Secondary Income Data

## 1. Thesis

The headline trade deficit hides a second, almost entirely outbound ledger: secondary income — mostly foreign remittances earned in America and sent abroad ($1.1 trillion cumulatively, $72 billion in 2024 alone) plus $280 billion of government transfers — which now fully negates the celebrated US services surplus. With primary income having rolled from surplus into slight deficit after 2017-2018, this steady secondary-income bleed widens the current-account deficit ($450 billion per quarter versus a $414 billion quarterly trade deficit), and its mirror-image capital-account surplus mechanically bids US financial assets and homes, worsening affordability and wealth inequality — making secondary income a bipartisan repair lever.

## 4B TAKE

- [TAKE] [15:02] Secondary income almost entirely offsets the services surplus that trade-deficit minimizers like to cite, so the services cushion is an illusion on a net basis.
- [TAKE] [03:53] Primary income no longer offsets the secondary bleed because its surplus rolled over after 2017-2018 into slight deficit, which makes secondary income structurally more important than before.
- [TAKE] [02:28] Every dollar of current-account deficit must return as a capital-and-financial-account surplus that mechanically bids Treasuries, homes, and US financial assets, which links cross-border flows directly to asset bubbles and inequality.
- [TAKE] [02:33] Home affordability tracks the capital-account surplus back to the 1980s except for the January 2012-January 2020 window when Fed MBS buying and QT broke the correlation, proving the flow-to-price channel.
- [TAKE] [17:44] Policymakers serious about the current-account deficit should target secondary income because the $414B-per-quarter trade gap will be an uphill fight while remittance and aid outflows are directly legible policy levers.
