# Breaking: $71 Billion Credit Plunge — Banks Out, Government In?

## 1. Thesis

August G.19 shows a $71.5 billion revolving deleveraging at a 5.5% annualized rate against still-positive total credit, and the balance of evidence points to consumers paying down and banks restraining rather than a charge-off wave: the 12-month revolving average still grows 2.6%, the card charge-off rate sits at 4.17% with delinquency at 3.0% on lagged Q1 data, and both have eased. The structural read is that private-bank credit creation broke in 1990 and again in 2008, leaving a $15 trillion consumer-credit gap and a $35 to $40 trillion loans-and-leases gap versus the 1950-1990 trend, so government money (M0/M2, Fed holdings at 15% of GDP) fills the void while households work 107 hours per year just to pay interest — with the 6-month rebound and M2 outpacing GDP hinting at early-cycle repair.

## 4B TAKE

- [TAKE] [02:56] The August deleveraging reflects paydown and restraint rather than defaults because charge-offs at 4.17% and delinquency at 3.0% eased on the latest available quarter.
- [TAKE] [03:19] The charge-off rate-of-change turn matches the stealth-recession template because a six-month spike followed by fading has marked every post-1990 early-cycle turn.
- [TAKE] [03:54] The 107-hour interest burden marks a breaking point because the same 105- to 110-hour level directly preceded the dot-com and 2008 recessions.
- [TAKE] [04:47] Twin deficits mechanically suppress personal saving because the current-account deficit plus fiscal deficits leave debt as the only net accumulation.
- [TAKE] [10:24] Private banks withdrawing since 1990 forces public money to fill the gap because 90% of dollars originate as bank credit, so M0/M2 and Fed-held debt at 15% of GDP rose as lending stalled.
