# Revolution at the Fed? - Pt 2

## 1. Thesis

Part two argues the reform menu associated with Malpass and Warsh — targeting real-wage growth and small-business credit while shrinking the Fed's footprint — is directionally right but internally contradictory. Both camps want lower rates with higher nominal growth, yet bond math says that combination requires forcing yields below their market level through QE or yield-curve control, which by definition delivers negative real rates, bondholder losses, and more asset inflation for the top 1%. With the deficit running 7 times its 2007 share, interest above $1 trillion per year near 5% of GDP, and the Fed balance sheet up from 6% to 22% of GDP, the path of least resistance is a repeat of the 1940s: keep debt cost below nominal growth via repression, effectively reversing the 1951 Treasury-Fed Accord and ending independence.

## 4B TAKE

- [TAKE] [10:21] Debt arithmetic only works when debt cost sits below nominal growth, so the 10-year pressing up to growth turns the fiscal position from relief to burden.
- [TAKE] [11:28] Suppressing yields with QE or curve control to cut interest expense directly inflates top-1% net worth and home unaffordability, which undercuts the help-the-bottom-half goal.
- [TAKE] [16:40] Lower rates plus higher nominal growth without coercion is impossible because bondholders demand higher coupons to offset faster purchasing-power loss.
- [TAKE] [23:52] Shrinking the Fed while expanding QE to hold rates down is mutually exclusive because repression requires a bigger balance sheet, not a smaller one.
- [TAKE] [28:40] Reversing the 1951 Accord into fiscal dominance ends independence because Treasury funding needs dictate the rate path rather than price stability.
