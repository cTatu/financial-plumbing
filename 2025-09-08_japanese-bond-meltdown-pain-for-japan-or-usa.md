# Japanese Bond Meltdown - Pain for Japan, or the USA?

## 1. Thesis

Japan's 30-year JGB yield setting all-time highs after the Prime Minister's resignation is reflation, not insolvency: inflation at 3.1% with 3.28% wage growth, corporate profits up from 2.5% to 6% of GDP, and a record 5% current-account surplus mean long yields are chasing nominal growth from a deeply depressed base. With 230% debt-to-GDP offset by Bank of Japan ownership of about half the stock with remitted interest, only 12-13% foreign-held JGBs, and a +90% of GDP creditor investment position, Japan can fund itself — the systemic risk sits in the United States, where more than $1 trillion of Japanese savings in Treasuries, equities, and real estate gets sold and repatriated into yen once domestic yields turn attractive.

## 4B TAKE

- [TAKE] [09:36] Rising JGB yields reflect improving nominal growth because 30-year yields remain below annual wage growth after 25 years near zero.
- [TAKE] [17:34] Japan's 230% debt load differs structurally from the United States because BoJ interest remittance zeroes half the burden while a 5% surplus funds the rest.
- [TAKE] [06:03] Yen strengthening alongside narrowing Japan-US spreads signals reflation rather than Liz Truss-style capital flight where currency and bonds fall together.
- [TAKE] [22:57] Repatriation is the US transmission because attractive JGBs incentivize selling Treasuries for dollars then selling dollars for yen, lifting US yields and weakening the dollar.
- [TAKE] [25:07] Japan retains a QE-or-currency choice because renewed yield-curve control would cap bond losses at the cost of yen weakness.

## 7. Follow-ups

- Watch JGB 30-year new highs with USD/JPY near 147.5: higher JGBs with yen stronger and US long yields higher confirms repatriation, while higher JGBs with yen sliding toward 161 says BoJ suppression is back.
