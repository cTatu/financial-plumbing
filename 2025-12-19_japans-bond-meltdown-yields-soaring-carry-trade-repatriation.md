# Japan's Bond Meltdown Sends Yields Soaring - Multi-Trillion Carry Trade & Capital Repatriation Risks

## 1. Thesis

The Bank of Japan hiked 25bp to 0.75%, the highest in 30 years, with more hikes signaled — yet the yen fell to about 157.5 per dollar and JGB yields kept climbing, with the 10-year breaking 2% and the 30-year at 3.42%, because dovish fiscal-populist policy plus 3% inflation dominates the rate signal. That ends the free-yen funding leg of a $7-8 trillion carry machine that borrowed yen to buy Treasuries and tech, and it opens the riskier second leg: Japanese capital repatriating home into now-positive domestic yields by selling Treasuries, selling dollars, and buying yen. With US 2-year and 10-year premia over Japan down to 2.5% and 2.1% and the US long end already rising into Fed cuts, losing the marginal Japanese buyer plus outright selling lands directly on US yields.

## 4B TAKE

- [TAKE] [01:44] Japan's 3% inflation against a 0.75% policy rate keeps real rates deeply negative, so one hike cannot offset the dovish fiscal-monetary mix driving yen weakness.
- [TAKE] [05:37] Rising yields mean falling bond prices, which repeats the duration trap that rendered US regional banks insolvent when long bonds had to be sold.
- [TAKE] [09:33] Free-to-paid yen funding ends the carry's first leg because borrowing at zero or negative rates to buy dollars, Treasuries, and tech no longer pencils at 2% domestic yields.
- [TAKE] [08:29] Repatriation reverses the flywheel because selling Treasuries into selling dollars into buying yen and JGBs lifts US yields while dragging the dollar lower.
- [TAKE] [11:54] Narrowing 2-year and 10-year premia remove the marginal buyer because 2.5% and 2.1% of carry compensates less for FX risk than the earlier 3.3-4.0% edge.
