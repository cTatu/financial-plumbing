# Russian Oil Sanctions = Bond Market Disaster?

## 1. Thesis

Trump's sanctions on two of Russia's largest producers covering 5.3 million barrels per day ignited a 10% two-day oil spike into a crowded short, and the presenter traces a two-legged bond-market threat: higher crude lifts CPI almost one-for-one and forces tighter policy or higher term premium, while energy-importing creditors from Japan to France face wider current-account deficits that leave fewer dollars for Treasuries just as the United States must fund about 7% of GDP in new debt each year. The dollar-oil link that flipped from negative to positive after 2019 on QT, hikes, exporter status, and haven demand could snap back if a 7% deficit with 23% of revenue going to interest leaves the Fed unable to hike, so early curve selling may be only the first repricing.

## 4B TAKE

- [TAKE] Higher oil lifts CPI because fuel powers factory production plus land, sea, and air transport that embeds energy in goods and services prices.
- [TAKE] Higher CPI pressures the Fed toward hikes and tighter policy, which lifts Treasury yields and lowers existing bond prices.
- [TAKE] Higher oil widens importer current-account deficits because Japan, India, France, and China must fund survival energy before buying interest-bearing dollars.
- [TAKE] Eroded foreign surpluses reduce Treasury demand into rising supply because 7%-of-GDP issuance must still clear while import bills consume dollars.
- [TAKE] The Fed may be unable to hike through an oil shock because a 7% deficit with 23% of revenue going to interest turns tighter policy into wider deficits and more supply.
