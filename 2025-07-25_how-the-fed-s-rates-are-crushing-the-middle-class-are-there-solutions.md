# How the Fed's Rates are Crushing the Middle Class - Are there solutions?

## 1. Thesis

With calls for Fed cuts rising, he made this video to show rate hikes land as a K-shaped squeeze rather than a shared slowdown: floating-rate debtors face doubled and tripled yearly interest with savings exhausted, while pre-2020 homeowners sit on termed-out low rates plus a $20.2T-to-$34T equity buffer. The proposed way out is not refinancing but owning assets whose appreciation outruns the $1,700-to-$3,300 per-person debt-service load, since wages at +585% have lost badly to interest at +1,300% since 1975.

- [12:37] Personal savings spiked to 24% of disposable income in April 2020 and then drew down to very low levels versus 14% in the mid-1970s. (as of Jul 2025)

- [15:23] Total homeowner equity rose from $20.2 trillion before the price run to $34 trillion now. (as of Jul 2025)
- [18:57] Since 1975 average hourly wages rose about 585% while interest payments per person rose about 1,300%, or 2.5 times faster. (as of Jul 2025)

## 4B TAKE

- [TAKE] [16:00] America splits into pre-2020 owners with locked low rates plus a $20.2T-to-$34T equity cushion and everyone else absorbing $27,000 mortgage interest and $1,500 card interest without a buffer.
- [TAKE] [07:34] Floating-rate cards transmit Fed hikes immediately while termed-out mortgages insulate incumbents, so card delinquency near 3% rises while mortgage delinquency stays near historic lows.
- [TAKE] [13:49] The 24% savings spike fully reversed within two years, so the $1,700-to-$3,300 jump in per-person interest now binds because the cash buffer is gone.
- [TAKE] [21:35] Assets that appreciate faster than debt-service deleverage the burden over decades, which is why S&P, gold, and Bitcoin ownership offsets the $1,700-to-$3,300 load while debt-only balance sheets compound it.
- [TAKE] [20:12] Total interest at 5% of wages sits at an all-time high that preceded the last three recessions, so it marks a breaking-point threshold rather than proof that a recession must follow.
