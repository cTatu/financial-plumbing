# The Petrodollar is Breaking, and it's Causing Bond Yields to Soar

## 1. Thesis

Bond yields are not soaring on inflation alone but on a breakdown in petrodollar recycling: the oil shock around $105/bbl plus Strait of Hormuz closure shifts the $3.3B/day incremental surplus away from GCC Treasury recyclers toward sanctioned or de-dollarizing producers that do not buy Western debt, while energy importers are pushed into wider current-account deficits that must be financed at higher rates — so bond-friendly demand shrinks at the same moment stressed holders like Japan must sell, lifting yields globally through arbitrage, a stronger dollar, and imported inflation.

## 4B TAKE

- [TAKE] [06:07] The petrodollar worked because Gulf producers earned dollars daily and recycled them into Treasuries in exchange for US security, so the identity of the exporter matters more than the gross dollar amount.
- [TAKE] [08:19] Importers like the UK, France, Japan, and Germany face an immediate terms-of-trade shock when oil rises, so their current accounts widen and they must offer higher rates to attract scarce foreign capital while growth tightens.
- [TAKE] [09:27] Surplus shifting to Russia, Brazil, Canada, and US domestic reinvestment shrinks bond-friendly demand because sanctioned, BRICS, or domestic producers do not recycle into Treasuries the way the GCC did.
- [TAKE] [14:01] Falling demand with steady Treasury supply means lower bond prices and higher yields, and cross-market arbitrage drags JGB, gilt, OAT, and Treasury yields higher together.
- [TAKE] [15:25] A stronger DXY then weakens importer currencies and imports more inflation, forcing central banks toward higher funding costs and ever tighter financial conditions.
